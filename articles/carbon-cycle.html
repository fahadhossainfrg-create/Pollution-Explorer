How Humans Disrupted a Cycle Essential to All Life — The Carbon Cycle in Detail

This video explains the carbon cycle as a movement of carbon between the atmosphere, living organisms, oceans, soils, sediments and rocks. Its key argument is that humans have not created a new carbon cycle; instead, humans have changed the speed and balance of an existing natural one.

Atmosphere, photosynthesis and food webs

The first part explains that plants and microorganisms take carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through photosynthesis. Using sunlight, they build organic molecules and store carbon in living tissue.

Animals acquire that carbon by eating plants or other animals. Respiration returns some carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. When organisms die, decomposition moves carbon into soils, sediments and the atmosphere. This creates a relatively fast biological cycle.

The ocean is part of the same system

The video then extends the cycle into the hydrosphere. Carbon dioxide moves between the atmosphere and ocean surface. Dissolved carbon is used by marine organisms, including plankton and organisms that build calcium-carbonate structures.

Carbon also reaches oceans through runoff and weathering. This is an important connection to marine environments because extra atmospheric carbon dioxide changes ocean chemistry and contributes to ocean acidification.

Geological storage and fossil carbon

Some carbon is removed from the fast biological cycle for extremely long periods. Organic matter can be buried, compressed and altered over geological time, eventually forming fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas.

This long-term storage is part of the lithosphere. Natural geological processes move carbon slowly between rocks, oceans and atmosphere.

Human disruption: changing the rate

The central human impact is the extraction and combustion of fossil carbon. Coal, oil and gas contain carbon that was stored underground for millions of years. Burning them transfers that carbon into the atmosphere over decades and centuries.

The problem is therefore partly a rate problem. Natural sinks such as oceans and vegetation absorb some extra carbon, but not fast enough to prevent atmospheric carbon dioxide from increasing.

Mitigation and carbon removal

The final section considers ways to reduce atmospheric carbon. Protecting and expanding vegetation, improving land management and developing technologies such as direct-air capture can remove some carbon dioxide.

However, the video stresses an important management principle: removing carbon is not a substitute for continuously adding large amounts of new carbon. Emission reduction remains central.
