Detailed video article

Dear Future Generations: Sorry — Environmental Responsibility and the Future

This video uses an imagined conversation with future generations to turn environmental change into a question of responsibility. Its main power comes from perspective: instead of asking only what is happening now, it asks what people living today may be remembered for.

An apology across generations

The speaker addresses people who have not yet been born and imagines apologising for the environmental conditions they may inherit. This creates the idea of intergenerational equity: the principle that present-day societies have responsibilities to people in the future.

The structure is important. The speaker does not begin with a scientific definition. He begins with regret. That makes the video persuasive rather than purely explanatory and helps explain why it is effective as a class discussion starter about environmental values.

The Amazon, forests and ecosystem services

A major section focuses on forests, especially the Amazon. Trees are discussed not simply as objects but as providers of ecosystem services. Forests store carbon, influence rainfall and water cycling, support biodiversity, protect soils and provide foods and materials.

The video criticises the idea that forests should be valued only after they are converted into money. This directly connects to the class comparison between human-centred and Earth-centred worldviews. The speaker is asking whether short-term economic value should outweigh long-term ecological function.

Pollution, species loss and the problem of short-term profit

The speech broadens from forests to polluted oceans, biodiversity loss and climate change. These problems are tied to consumption, political priorities and business decisions. The repeated theme is that economic activity becomes destructive when environmental costs are ignored.

For Geography, this is useful because it introduces the concept of externalities: costs such as pollution, habitat damage or declining water quality may be imposed on communities or ecosystems even when they are not included in the market price of a product.

The ending changes the message

The video does not finish by accepting the imagined damaged future. Its final movement argues that present choices can still change outcomes. This shift is important because environmental management is not presented as hopeless.

The video therefore works best as a bridge between environmental change and management. It identifies problems, questions values and then asks what actions — individual, political and institutional — could change the trajectory.

Evidence check / updated context

The video was released in 2015 and contains dramatic environmental statistics intended for persuasion. Those numbers should not automatically be treated as current. In this project, newer pollution statistics come from WHO, Australian government agencies and other contemporary sources.

Study and assessment questions
