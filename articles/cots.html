Crown-of-Thorns Starfish — Reef Ecology, Outbreaks and Management

Crown-of-thorns starfish are native coral predators on the Great Barrier Reef. The management problem is not their simple existence; it is the development of large outbreaks that can remove living coral faster than reefs can recover.

Why crown-of-thorns starfish matter

Crown-of-thorns starfish feed on living coral tissue. In low numbers, predation is part of reef ecology. During outbreaks, however, very high densities can cause rapid coral loss.

That matters because coral provides physical habitat for many reef organisms. A major decline in coral cover can therefore affect biodiversity, fisheries, tourism and the reef’s ability to recover from other disturbances.

Why outbreaks are complex

The class question asks how humans can contribute to COTS outbreaks. A careful answer avoids a single-cause explanation. Major flood events can carry excess nutrients from agricultural and developed catchments into reef waters. Nutrient availability may increase the survival of COTS larvae under some conditions.

Other factors also matter, including currents, coral availability, natural predator abundance and the timing of larval development. Human activity can influence outbreak conditions without being the only cause.

How management works

The current control program combines surveillance with targeted culling. Reefs are monitored, priority sites are identified and trained divers remove or lethally inject COTS.

Management is selective because the Great Barrier Reef is enormous. It is not practical or ecologically desirable to try to remove every COTS. The goal is to protect important coral areas and increase reef resilience while broader water-quality and climate pressures are also addressed.

Why this case is useful in Geography

COTS demonstrates interconnection. Nutrients may begin in the lithosphere on farms, travel through the hydrosphere as runoff, influence plankton in the marine environment and affect coral and starfish in the biosphere.

It therefore links water pollution, food webs, catchment management and marine ecosystem health in a single Australian case study.
