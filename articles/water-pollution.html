Water Pollution

How sewage, nutrients, chemicals, plastics and sediment enter water, move through catchments, damage ecosystems and threaten human health.

Pollution typeGlobal + Australian perspective
All articlesPollution Explorer
Definition: Water pollution occurs when biological, chemical or physical contaminants change water quality enough to harm organisms, ecosystems or human uses.

Where it comes from

Point sources have an identifiable outlet, such as a wastewater pipe, mine discharge or factory drain. Diffuse sources are spread across a landscape: fertiliser, pesticides, animal waste, litter, oil and sediment are washed from farms and cities during rain. Sewage introduces pathogens and oxygen-demanding organic matter; industry and mining can release metals and persistent chemicals; plastics fragment into microplastics.

How pollution spreads

Rain and irrigation move contaminants across land into drains, streams and rivers. Rivers connect inland catchments with estuaries and oceans, while dissolved chemicals may seep into groundwater. Currents transport pollutants along coasts. Some contaminants settle in sediment and later return to the water when storms, dredging or animals disturb the bed. Others accumulate in organisms and become more concentrated through food webs.

Environmental and wildlife impacts

Excess nitrogen and phosphorus can cause algal blooms. When algae die, decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic conditions in which fish and invertebrates struggle to survive. Sediment reduces light for aquatic plants and can smother coral or spawning habitat. Toxic chemicals can impair growth, reproduction and behaviour. Plastic can entangle wildlife or be swallowed, while pathogens can make habitats and seafood unsafe.

Human impacts

Unsafe water can transmit diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio. WHO estimates that microbiologically contaminated drinking water causes about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths each year. Pollution also raises treatment costs, closes beaches and fisheries, affects recreation and can reduce access to culturally important waterways.

Australia lens

Australian water pollution varies by place. Urban stormwater carries litter, nutrients, tyre particles and oils into creeks and harbours. Agricultural runoff can move soil, nutrients and pesticides through river systems and toward the Great Barrier Reef. Acid mine drainage and legacy contamination affect some mining regions. During drought, lower river flow may reduce dilution; intense rainfall and floods can rapidly transport pollutants across catchments.

Solutions

The strongest response begins at the source: reliable sanitation and wastewater treatment, industrial discharge controls, careful fertiliser application, erosion prevention and reduced plastic use. Wetlands, riparian vegetation, rain gardens and gross-pollutant traps slow runoff and capture contaminants. Monitoring, catchment-wide planning and Indigenous-led water management help identify risks and protect ecological and cultural values.

Key idea: Pollution rarely stays where it begins. Managing a beach, river or reef requires action across the entire upstream catchment.
