Soil Pollution

Why contaminated land is often a hidden problem—and how pollutants move from soil into water, crops, wildlife and people.

Pollution typeFood security + Australia
All articlesPollution Explorer
Definition: Soil pollution is the presence of substances or wastes at concentrations that damage soil functions, organisms, plants, water quality or human health.

Sources and types

Common sources include industrial waste, mining and smelting, leaking fuel tanks, landfill, sewage sludge, excessive pesticides and fertilisers, e-waste, demolition material and historical use of lead-based products. Important contaminant groups include metals such as lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic; petroleum hydrocarbons; pesticides; persistent organic pollutants; excess nutrients; salts; and microplastics.

How it spreads

Contaminants may bind to soil particles, dissolve in soil water or be taken up by plants and soil organisms. Wind can carry contaminated dust. Rain can wash polluted sediment into waterways or leach soluble chemicals toward groundwater. Crops and animals can transfer contaminants into food chains. Whether a pollutant moves depends on soil type, acidity, organic matter, rainfall and the chemical itself.

Environmental and wildlife impacts

Healthy soil is a living ecosystem. Pollution can reduce microbial diversity, harm earthworms and other decomposers, disrupt nutrient cycling and weaken plant growth. It may reduce soil fertility and water-holding capacity. Contaminants entering plants or prey species can affect insects, birds and mammals, while polluted sediment can damage freshwater and marine ecosystems.

Human health and food security

People may be exposed by swallowing dust, breathing particles, touching contaminated soil, drinking affected groundwater or eating contaminated crops and animals. Children are particularly vulnerable to lead in soil and household dust. FAO and UNEP report that soil pollution reduces the soil’s ability to provide ecosystem services and threatens production of safe and sufficient food.

Australia lens

Australia has contaminated sites linked to mining, smelting, fuel storage, firefighting foams, former gasworks, industry and older urban development. Lead exposure remains a concern around historic smelting and mining communities. Acid sulfate soils can release acid and metals when drained or disturbed. Regulation is mainly managed by states and territories, with contaminated-land registers, investigation standards and site-specific remediation.

Prevention and remediation

Prevention includes safer chemical design, secure waste storage, integrated pest management, spill control and testing land before sensitive uses such as housing or schools. Remediation can remove and safely contain soil, isolate it beneath clean cover, wash or stabilise contaminants, use microbes to break down organic chemicals, or use selected plants to extract or immobilise pollutants. The best method depends on risk, land use, cost and whether treatment creates another waste stream.

Key idea: Contamination and exposure are not identical. Risk depends on the pollutant, its concentration, how people or wildlife can contact it, and the sensitivity of the receptor.
