Noise Pollution

How unwanted sound is measured, how it travels, and why traffic, aircraft, industry and leisure noise affect health and wildlife.

Definition: Noise pollution is unwanted or harmful sound that interferes with health, communication, learning, sleep, wildlife behaviour or the valued character of a place.

Sources and measurement

Major sources include road and rail traffic, aircraft, construction, industry, machinery, amplified music and neighbourhood activity. Sound level is commonly measured in decibels. Because the decibel scale is logarithmic, a small numerical increase can represent a large rise in sound energy. A-weighted decibels, written dB(A), approximate the sensitivity of human hearing, but a complete assessment also considers duration, frequency, timing, repetition and background sound.

How noise spreads

Sound travels as pressure waves through air, water and solid materials. It weakens with distance, but wind and temperature layers can bend sound, while hard surfaces reflect it. Buildings, earth mounds and vegetation belts can block or scatter some sound. Low-frequency noise often travels farther and passes through structures more easily than high-frequency sound.

Human impacts

Very loud sound can damage hearing. Chronic environmental noise can also disturb sleep, increase annoyance and stress, interfere with conversation and learning, and contribute to cardiovascular and metabolic effects. WHO recommends less than 30 dB(A) in bedrooms at night for good-quality sleep and less than 35 dB(A) in classrooms for suitable teaching and learning conditions. Individual response varies, so average levels do not capture every impact.

Wildlife impacts

Animals use sound to find mates, defend territory, detect predators, coordinate groups and navigate. Human noise can mask these signals. Wildlife may abandon habitat, change calling behaviour, spend less time feeding or experience stress. Underwater noise from ships, sonar, construction and seismic surveys can affect whales, dolphins, fish and other marine life across large areas.

Australia lens

Australian communities experience road, rail, aircraft, construction, industrial and entertainment noise. Responsibility is shared among councils, state environment and planning agencies, transport authorities and workplace regulators. Planning can separate sensitive land uses from major noise sources, while curfews, operating conditions and project assessments manage timing and exposure. Sydney Airport and expanding transport corridors illustrate the trade-off between mobility, economic activity and residential amenity.

Solutions: follow the pathway

At the source: quieter vehicles and equipment, maintenance, speed management and limits on operating hours. Along the path: barriers, building orientation, earth mounds and distance. At the receiver: insulation, double glazing and hearing protection. Source control usually protects more people; receiver protection is important but should not carry the entire burden.

Key idea: Noise is both physical and contextual. The same measured level may have different consequences depending on whether it occurs in a classroom, hospital, home, habitat or industrial workplace.
