Australia lens • 2026 update

Pollution in the Australian context

Australia has distinctive pollution pathways: bushfire and dust smoke, large urban and industrial airsheds, agricultural catchments draining to the Great Barrier Reef, legacy contaminated land and PFAS, jurisdiction-specific noise controls, highly light-sensitive wildlife, and planning systems that manage visual amenity rather than using one national “visual pollution” index.

🇦🇺

How Australia manages pollution

Australia often combines national standards or guidance with implementation by states and territories, plus local planning and nuisance controls. The exact legal rule therefore depends on the pollutant and location. This section labels national guidance separately from state/local examples.

Sources & data notes

Statistics are dated and linked to their original or high-authority sources. Visual pollution is less standardized than the other categories, so the site uses a systematic review and city regulation case study rather than claiming a global ranking.

[1] WHO — Air pollution overview. 99% global exposure statement; definition and major pollutants.
WHO air pollution
[2] WHO — Air pollutants and health burden. 6.7 million premature deaths annually from ambient + household air pollution; 4.2 million attributed to ambient air pollution.
WHO pollutant types
[3] WHO/UNICEF JMP — Progress on household drinking water, sanitation and hygiene 2000–2024. 2.1 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water in 2024.
UNICEF JMP 2025 report
[4] UN-Water / WHO — Wastewater treatment, 2024 update. 2022 household wastewater treatment estimate and data limitations.
UN-Water wastewater update
[5] FAO & UNEP — Global Assessment of Soil Pollution. Drivers, pathways, food-system and ecosystem risks.
FAO soil pollution
[6] China Ministry of Ecology and Environment — National soil survey. 2014 survey: 16.1% of sampling sites exceeded soil environmental standards; this is a site exceedance rate, not the share of national land area polluted.
China soil survey
[7] European Environment Agency — Environmental noise in Europe 2025. Exposure, transport sources and health burden.
EEA noise report
[8] Falchi et al., Science Advances (2016) — World Atlas of artificial night sky brightness. Global and country-level light-pollution estimates, including Singapore.
World Atlas paper
[9] Kyba et al., Science (2023). Citizen observations suggest an average 9.6% annual increase in visible-band sky brightness from 2011–2022 at observation locations.
Science sky-brightness study
[10] São Paulo City — Clean City Law (Lei 14.223/2006). Official city regulation aimed at organizing the urban landscape and combating visual pollution.
São Paulo Clean City Law
[11] Gao et al., Land (2024) — Systematic review of visual pollution. Finds no unified definition or standardized measurement method; reviews 52 studies.
Visual pollution review
[12] Government of India, Press Information Bureau — National Clean Air Programme. Official NCAP target: up to 40% PM10 reduction or national standard by 2025–26 in 130 cities as reported in November 2024.
India NCAP
[13] WHO — Health effects of air pollution: evidence and implications (2026). WHO technical brief reporting about 6.6 million deaths globally each year and identifying vulnerable groups including children, pregnant women, older people and people with chronic disease.
WHO 2026 air pollution technical brief
[14] WHO — Arsenic fact sheet. Global groundwater exposure, health effects, 10 μg/L provisional guideline and Bangladesh burden information.
WHO arsenic fact sheet
[15] WHO — Guidance on environmental noise. Health outcomes include annoyance, sleep disturbance, ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, hearing impairment, tinnitus and cognitive effects.
WHO environmental noise guidance
[16] WHO — Improving water safety. Water safety plans, safely managed services and risk management from catchment to consumer.
WHO water safety
[17] Australian Institute of Health and Welfare — Natural environment and health (2026). Reports that ambient PM₂.₅ accounted for 1.6% of Australia’s total burden of disease in 2024; also summarizes Black Summer health impacts.
AIHW natural environment and health
[18] Australia State of the Environment 2021 — Air quality. Australian air trends, 2019–20 bushfire smoke, Canberra PM₂.₅ episode, monitoring and pollutant sources.
State of the Environment — outdoor air quality
[19] DCCEEW — Particulate matter (PM₁₀ and PM₂.₅). Current Australian national ambient standards listed by DCCEEW: PM₂.₅ 25 µg/m³ over 24 hours and 8 µg/m³ annually; PM₁₀ 50 µg/m³ over 24 hours and 25 µg/m³ annually.
DCCEEW particulate matter
[20] Reef 2050 Catchment Water Quality Strategy — Targets and indicators (2026). Uses a 2023 baseline and sets Reef-wide anthropogenic load-reduction targets for DIN, sediment, particulate nitrogen and phosphorus, plus a pesticide-protection target.
Reef water-quality targets
[21] NHMRC — Australian Drinking Water Guidelines, PFAS update (2025). Health-based guideline values: PFOS 8 ng/L, PFOA 200 ng/L, PFHxS 30 ng/L and PFBS 1000 ng/L.
Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — PFAS
[22] Australian and New Zealand Guidelines for Fresh and Marine Water Quality. Authoritative framework for ecosystem water-quality management, including physical/chemical stressors, toxicants and cultural/spiritual values.
ANZ Fresh and Marine Water Quality Guidelines

Assessment of Site Contamination NEPM

PFAS NEMP 3.1
